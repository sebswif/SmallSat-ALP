\section{Introduction}
% Student source: Draft 1 -- AE 298 RES, by Roshni Kankanala.
For years, scientists have tried to understand the nature of dark matter, an elusive component of the cosmic mass budget that shapes galaxies, yet remains a mystery and is almost entirely invisible to our most advanced telescopes. Dark matter is theorized to give galaxies mass, generating extra gravity that prevents them from tearing apart due to their rotational speeds\cite{cernDarkMatter}. Most dark matter searches operate underground, such as the SuperCDMS and LUX-ZEPLIN experiments, but the Earth's atmosphere and crust can shield or scatter some faint dark matter signals~\cite{alpine2025darkness}.

Charge-Coupled Devices (CCDs) are silicon-based detectors that detect ionizing radiation and convert it into digital signals or a quantitative dataset. These devices are based on the photoelectric effect. The surface of a CCD is divided into squares called “pixels” and when a photon strikes a pixel, it knocks the electrons free, after which these charges are measured and converted into an image. Skipper-CCDs are an advanced version of traditional CCDs and have significantly less readout noise compared to standard CCDs. In a standard CCD, the pixel charge is dumped onto the output node, measured once and drained, losing the charge packet. However, a Skipper CCD has a modified output stage that allows this charge to be measured and “skipped” back and forth for repeated, non-destructive, and independent measurements. Averaging these measurements supports searches for low-energy ionization and X-ray events~\cite{alpine2025darkness}.

DarkNESS is a 6U CubeSat deployed in Low Earth Orbit (LEO) that serves as both a targeted dark matter search and a technology demonstration for Skipper-CCDs in the space environment. It aims to detect dark matter through Skipper-CCDs, which have demonstrated laboratory sensitivity to X-rays from hypothetical dark matter decay. Operating Skipper-CCDs in LEO avoids Earth's shielding effects that limit underground experiments and is useful for observing X-ray signals and dark matter model signals, which are attenuated in Earth's atmosphere before reaching underground instruments. The published design has two main scientific objectives\cite{alpine2025darkness}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Search for strongly interacting sub-GeV dark matter.}
    The CubeSat targets the constellation Cygnus, which lies near the direction of the solar system’s motion through the galactic dark matter halo, effectively flying into a dark matter “wind”. DarkNESS looks for a distinct, predictable daily variation in signal rate and uses the Earth’s shadow as a shielding mechanism to ensure this predictable variation.

  \item \textbf{Search for X-rays from dark matter decay.}
    Sterile neutrinos and other dark matter candidates in the keV mass scale are hypothesized to decay into an active neutrino and a monoenergetic X-ray photon. The CubeSat points towards the Galactic Center (Sagittarius A* region), where dark matter density is the highest. Observations happen during orbital umbra to eliminate solar X-ray background contamination \cite{alpine2025darkness}.
\end{enumerate}

This paper separately considers two possible source channels for axion-like particles (ALPs), hypothetical pseudoscalar particles that may be connected to dark matter and early-universe physics. The cosmological channel is treated here as a diffuse flux of ALPs arriving from cosmological distances; the solar channel is a directional flux of ALPs produced in the Sun. In both channels, ALPs may "convert" into X-ray photons through the inverse Primakoff effect in Earth's magnetic field\cite{yamamoto2020alp,davoudiasl2008gecosax}. The channels differ in their origin and expected sky distribution but share the same conversion mechanism. Solar ALPs need not constitute the Galactic dark matter population.

The purpose of this paper is to contribute to the following research question:

\begin{quote}
``Which astrophysical ALP source channels can a LEO X-ray observatory identify through conversion in Earth's magnetic field, and what instrument and observing strategy make those signals measurable against particle and diffuse X-ray backgrounds?''
\end{quote}

This work will use DarkNESS as an available platform to assess the feasibility of a third dark matter objective involving ALPs. This dark matter channel and its accessibility by an observatory-class CubeSat will be explored by four specific guiding questions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item How feasible is a cosmological ALP source channel for a LEO CubeSat using Skipper-CCDs, assuming conversion in Earth's magnetic field?
  \item How feasible is a solar ALP source channel for a LEO CubeSat using Skipper-CCDs, assuming conversion in Earth's magnetic field?
  \item How should the CubeSat's pointing strategy and observing constraints be set to observe whichever ALP channel is feasible?
  \item How should the CubeSat monitor backgrounds, including with proxies, control exposures, and a particle monitor, to distinguish a feasible channel's signal?
\end{enumerate}

To address these questions, this paper evaluates the theoretical and operational feasibility of detecting ALPs from LEO. First, analysis of the expected X-ray signals is performed for both cosmological and solar ALP channels using the inverse Primakoff effect to determine which source produces a viable signal within the threshold for detection of Skipper-CCDs. Based on the channel selected, the study drafts an optimized orbital pointing strategy that uses the Earth's magnetic field  and orbital geometry to maximize the detection probability of ALPs. 

\section{Science and Background}
\subsection{ALPs and Inverse Primakoff Effect}

ALPs differ significantly from the standard Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) axions, which were introduced to solve the strong Charge Parity (CP) problem. ALPs are the most common example of Pseudo-Nambu-Goldstone bosons (pNGBs), which are 0-spin bosons with a minuscule mass created due to symmetry breaking at high energy scales. As bosons, they have integral spins and do not obey the Pauli exclusion principle. Their interactions are defined by an effective Lagrangian, a mathematical piece of field theory that allows an ALP to convert into a photon when exposed to an external magnetic field through the inverse Primakoff effect \cite{chakraborti2026probing}. The Lagrangian is defined as 

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} \supset -\frac{1}{4} g_{a\gamma} a F_{\mu\nu} \tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}.
\end{equation}

In this equation, $\mathcal{L}$ represents the effective interaction Lagrangian density, which describes the interaction between the axion or axion-like particle (ALP) field, denoted by $a$, and the electromagnetic field. The strength of this interaction is determined by the axion-photon coupling constant, $g_{a\gamma}$. The electromagnetic field itself is represented by the field tensor $F_{\mu\nu}$, while $\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}$ denotes the dual electromagnetic field tensor. Finally, the subscripts and superscripts $\mu$ and $\nu$ serve as spacetime indices.

An ALP with a photon coupling can "convert" into a photon in a transverse magnetic field, approximately preserving its energy. The reference calculation assumes weak mixing, negligible absorption, and coherent conversion along the accepted ray\cite{yamamoto2020alp,davoudiasl2008gecosax}.
