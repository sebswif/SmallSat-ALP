\section{Introduction}
% Student source: Draft 1 -- AE 298 RES, by Roshni Kankanala.
For years, scientists have tried to understand the nature of dark matter, an influential component that shapes galaxies, yet remains a mystery and is entirely invisible to our most advanced telescopes. This dark matter is theorized to give galaxies extra mass, generating extra gravity that prevents them from tearing apart due to their rotational speeds\cite{cernDarkMatter}.

The DarkNESS CubeSat mission aims to detect this dark matter through Skipper Charge Coupled Devices (CCDs), which have demonstrated laboratory sensitivity to X-rays from hypothetical dark matter decay. DarkNESS is a 6U CubeSat deployed in Low Earth Orbit (LEO) that serves as both a targeted search for dark matter and a technology demonstration for Skipper CCDs. The main scientific objectives of this mission are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Detection of strongly interacting sub-GeV dark matter.}
  The CubeSat targets the constellation Cygnus, which lies near the direction of the solar system's motion through the galactic dark matter halo, effectively flying into a dark matter ``wind''. DarkNESS looks for a distinct, predictable daily variation in signal rate and uses the Earth's shadow as a shielding mechanism to ensure this predictable variation.

  \item \textbf{Detection of X-ray signatures of dark matter.}
  Sterile neutrinos and other dark matter candidates in the keV mass scale are hypothesized to decay into an active neutrino and a monoenergetic X-ray photon. The CubeSat points towards the Galactic Center (Sagittarius A* region), where dark matter density is the highest. Observations happen during orbital umbra to eliminate solar X-ray background contamination\cite{alpine2025darkness}.
\end{enumerate}

An axion-like particle (ALP) is a hypothetical pseudo particle with possible connection to dark matter and early universe physics. ALPs can be ``converted'' into photons via interactions with magnetic fields through the inverse Primakoff effect\cite{yamamoto2020alp}.

Charge Coupled Devices (CCDs) are silicon-based detectors which detect photons/ionizing radiation and convert it into a digital image or a quantitative dataset. These devices are primarily based on the photoelectric effect. The surface of a CCD is divided into squares called ``pixels'' and when a photon strikes a pixel, it knocks the electrons free, after which these charges are measured and converted into an image. Skipper CCDs are an advanced version of traditional CCDs and have significantly less readout noise compared to standard CCDs. In a standard CCD, the pixel charge is dumped onto the output node, measured once and wiped. However, a Skipper CCD has a modified output stage that allows this charge to be measured and ``skipped'' back and forth to be measured again, non-destructively.

The purpose of this paper is to answer this research question:

\begin{quote}
``Using the DarkNESS platform, what observing ConOps maximizes the identifiable geomagnetic ALP--photon conversion signal against particle and diffuse X-ray backgrounds, and what does that ConOps require of the mission?''
\end{quote}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Which pointing law keeps the most conversion information after the background regression, under radiator, limb, slew and telemetry constraints?
  \item How much is gained over the nominal sterile-neutrino (Galactic Centre) pointing?
  \item What does umbra-only versus sunlit imaging cost, and what evidence would justify sunlit operation?
  \item What background monitoring (proxies, control exposures, particle monitor) keeps the signal identifiable?
\end{enumerate}
