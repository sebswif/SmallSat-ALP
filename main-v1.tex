\section{Introduction}

For years, scientists have tried to understand the nature of dark matter, an elusive component of the cosmic mass budget that shapes galaxies, yet remains a mystery and is almost entirely invisible to our most advanced telescopes. Dark matter is theorized to give galaxies mass, generating extra gravity that prevents them from tearing apart due to their rotational speeds\cite{cernDarkMatter}. Most dark matter searches operate underground, such as the SuperCDMS and LUX-ZEPLIN experiments, but the Earth's atmosphere and crust can scatter or shield some faint dark matter signals~\cite{alpine2025darkness}.

Charge-Coupled Devices (CCDs) are silicon-based detectors that detect ionizing radiation and convert it into digital signals or a quantitative dataset. These devices are based on the photoelectric effect. The surface of a CCD is divided into squares called “pixels” and when a photon strikes a pixel, it knocks the electrons free, after which these charges are measured and converted into an image. Skipper-CCDs are an advanced version of traditional CCDs and have significantly less readout noise compared to standard CCDs. In a standard CCD, the pixel charge is dumped onto the output node, measured once and drained, losing the charge packet. However, a Skipper CCD has a modified output stage that allows this charge to be measured and “skipped” back and forth for repeated, non-destructive, and independent measurements. Averaging these measurements supports searches for low-energy ionization and X-ray events~\cite{alpine2025darkness}.

DarkNESS is a 6U CubeSat deployed in Low Earth Orbit (LEO) that serves as both a targeted dark matter search and a technology demonstration for Skipper-CCDs in the space environment. It aims to detect dark matter through Skipper-CCDs, which have demonstrated laboratory sensitivity to X-rays from hypothetical dark matter decay. Operating Skipper-CCDs in LEO avoids Earth's shielding effects that limit underground experiments and is useful for observing X-ray signals and dark matter model signals, which are attenuated in Earth's atmosphere before reaching underground instruments. The published design has two main scientific objectives\cite{alpine2025darkness}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Search for strongly interacting sub-GeV dark matter.}

  \item \textbf{Search for X-rays from dark matter decay.}
    \cite{alpine2025darkness}.
\end{enumerate}

Although the DarkNESS detector configuration is suitable for its proposed scientific objectives, its wide field of view and non-focusing design presents limitations for other astrophysical observations. The CubeSat's bare detectors observe a large area without using focusing instruments to concentrate signals at one point on the detector. A bare detector is useful when searching for diffuse signals distributed over a large section of the sky, but it also allows photons from unrelated astrophysical sources to contribute to the measured background. There are other branches of dark matter theory that could benefit from an optical design, as it collects photons over a large field of view and concentrates them onto a smaller detector region. 

One possible application of an optical design is the search for axion-like particles (ALPs), which are hypothetical pseudoscalar particles that may be connected to dark matter and early-universe physics. Depending on their properties, ALPs may contribute to dark matter, be produced within astrophysical environments, or generate observable photons through interactions with electromagnetic fields. Studies have investigated the contribution of decaying ALPs to the cosmic radiation background and the possibility of detecting solar axions through their conversion into X-rays in the Earth's magnetic field \cite{porrasbedmar2024decaying,davoudiasl2008gecosax}.

The purpose of this paper is to investigate the feasibility of adapting a Skipper-CCD CubeSat architecture for astrophysical ALP observations, with an emphasis on the use of X-ray optics and geomagnetic conversion. Two possible source channels are considered: a cosmological channel associated with ALPs originating outside the solar system and a solar channel associated with ALPs produced in the Sun. These channels differ in their expected energy distributions, arrival directions, and underlying production mechanisms. Their differences play an important role when performing a trade study between bare detectors and detectors with optics.  

This study addresses the following research question:

\begin{quote}
``Which astrophysical ALP source channels can a LEO X-ray observatory identify through conversion in Earth's magnetic field, and what instrument and observing strategy make those signals measurable against particle and diffuse X-ray backgrounds?''
\end{quote}

 The dark matter channel and its accessibility by an observatory-class CubeSat will be explored by four specific guiding questions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item How feasible is a cosmological ALP source channel for a LEO CubeSat using Skipper-CCDs, assuming conversion in Earth's magnetic field?
  \item How feasible is a solar ALP source channel for a LEO CubeSat using Skipper-CCDs, assuming conversion in Earth's magnetic field?
  \item How should the CubeSat's pointing strategy and observing constraints be set to observe whichever ALP channel is feasible?
  \item How should the CubeSat monitor backgrounds, including with proxies, control exposures, and a particle monitor, to distinguish a feasible channel's signal?
\end{enumerate}

To address these questions, this paper evaluates the theoretical and operational feasibility of detecting ALPs from LEO. First, analysis of the expected X-ray signals is performed for both cosmological and solar ALP channels using the inverse Primakoff effect to determine which source produces a viable signal within the threshold for detection of Skipper-CCDs. Based on the channel selected, the study drafts an optimized orbital pointing strategy that uses the Earth's magnetic field  and orbital geometry to maximize the detection probability of ALPs. 

\section{Science Motivation}

\subsection{Axion-Like Particles (ALPs)}

ALPs differ significantly from the standard Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) axions, which were introduced to solve the strong Charge Parity (CP) problem. ALPs are the most common example of Pseudo-Nambu-Goldstone bosons (pNGBs), which are 0-spin bosons with a minuscule mass created due to symmetry breaking at high energy scales. As bosons, they have integral spins and do not obey the Pauli exclusion principle. Their masses and interaction strengths can vary over a wide parameter space, allowing different ALP models to produce different astrophysical signatures. Some sufficiently stable ALPs could contribute to the dark matter population, while others may be produced in stars or decay into photons over cosmological timescales \cite{chakraborti2026probing, porrasbedmar2024decaying}. 

One of the most important properties of ALPs is their possible coupling to photons. This interaction provides a mechanism through which ALPs can be produced from photons, converted back into photons, or decay into photon pairs. Since ALPs are weakly interacting particles in many models, direct detection can be difficult. However, the photons involved with these processes may provide an indirect method of proving their existence. 

The mass of an ALP, denoted by $m_a$, and its coupling to photons, denoted by $g_{a\gamma}$, are two important parameters used to determine the expected signal. The mass determines how easily an ALP can convert into a photon while traveling through a magnetic field and the coupling constant controls the strength of the interaction. A smaller coupling generally produces lower probability of conversion, making the resulting photon signal more difficult to distinguish from background radiation \cite{chakraborti2026probing, davoudiasl2008gecosax}. 

\subsection{Inverse Primakoff Effect}

The interactions between an ALP and the electromagnetic field are defined by an effective Lagrangian, a mathematical piece of field theory that allows an ALP to "convert" into a photon when exposed to an external magnetic field through the inverse Primakoff effect. The Lagrangian is defined as 

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} \supset -\frac{1}{4} g_{a\gamma} a F_{\mu\nu} \tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}.
\end{equation}

In this equation, $\mathcal{L}$ represents the effective interaction Lagrangian density, which describes the interaction between the axion or axion-like particle (ALP) field, denoted by $a$, and the electromagnetic field. The strength of this interaction is determined by the axion-photon coupling constant, $g_{a\gamma}$. The electromagnetic field itself is represented by the field tensor $F_{\mu\nu}$, while $\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}$ denotes the dual electromagnetic field tensor. Finally, the subscripts and superscripts $\mu$ and $\nu$ serve as spacetime indices \cite{chakraborti2026probing}\textbf{}. 

 An ALP with a photon coupling can "convert" into a photon in a transverse magnetic field, approximately preserving its energy. The reference calculation assumes weak mixing, negligible absorption, and coherent conversion along the accepted ray\cite{yamamoto2020alp,davoudiasl2008gecosax}

 The Earth's magnetic field offers a naturallly occuring environment for ALP-photon conversion. Although weaker than the fields generated by laboratory magnets, it extends over a significantly larger distance. An ALP traveling through this region may therefore have a non-zero probability of converting into a photon before reaching a detector \cite{davoudiasl2008gecosax}. For a simplified case involving a uniform transverse magnetic field, negligible absorption, and weak ALP-photon mixing, the conversion probability can be written as
 \begin{equation}
P_{a\rightarrow\gamma}
=
\left(\frac{g_{a\gamma}B_TL}{2}\right)^2
\left[
\frac{1-\cos(qL)}{(qL/2)^2}
\right],
\end{equation}

where $B_T$ is the magnetic-field component perpendicular to the direction of propagation, $L$ is the conversion path length. The ALP-photon momentum transfer is defined as 

\begin{equation}
q \simeq \frac{m_a^2}{2E_a},
\end{equation}

where $E_a$ is the ALP energy. When $qL << 1$, the probability reduces to 

\begin{equation}
P_{a\rightarrow\gamma}
\simeq
\left(\frac{g_{a\gamma}B_TL}{2}\right)^2.
\end{equation}

This expression shows that the conversion probability increases with the square of the coupling strength, transverse magnetic field and line of  sight or path length. However, the actual geomagnetic field is not uniform and its direction and magnitude vary along the particle's trajectory. Atmospheric absorption and the changing spacecraft position must be factored in when calculating the observable signal \cite{yamamoto2020alp}.

The 2008 feasibility study of geomagnetic conversion of solar axions into X-rays, commonly referred to as GECOSAX, investigated these effects. the analysis included spacecraft orbits, the spatial variation of Earth's magnetic field, atmospheric attenuation, and measured X-ray backgrounds. The study demonstrated that the geomagnetic conversion could, in theory, provide sensitivity to weak axion-photon couplings  using an orbiting X-ray telescope \cite{davoudiasl2008gecosax}.

For a CubeSat, the importance of this mechanism is that the Earth's magnetic field acts as the conversion region, eliminating the need for large laboratory magnets. The spacecraft would instead require an X-ray detector capable of observing converted photons, along with a pointing strategy that selects favorable conversion geometries. However, the relatively small conversion probability means that the detector area and background filtering become major considerations in determining whether the signal is measurable.

\section{Cosmological and Solar ALP Channels}

ALPs may originate from different astrophysical environments, and the properties of their resuting signals depend on their production mechanisms and interactions with electromagnetic fields. This study considers two possible source channels: cosmological and solar. The differences in their expected energy distributions, arrival directions and detection mechanisms are important when determining which channel is better suited for observation using a CubeSat equipped with Skipper-CCDs and potentially, optics. 

\subsection{Cosmological ALP Channel}

The cosmological channel considers ALPs that may have been produced in the early universe or through distant astrophysical processes. Depending on their mass, lifetime, and interaction strength, these particles may contribute to the dark matter population or produce observable radiation through their decay into photons. One possible signature is an additional contribution to  the cosmic background, which consists of diffuse electromagnetic radiation originating from outside the Milky Way. 

Upon investigating the contribution of decaying ALP dark matter to the cosmic background, particularly across optical and X-ray energy bands, a model was proposed. In this model, ALPs decay into two photons, producing radiation that contributes to the observed cosmic background.
