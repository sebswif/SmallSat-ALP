\section{Introduction}
% Student source: Draft 1 -- AE 298 RES, by Roshni Kankanala.
For years, scientists have tried to understand the nature of dark matter, an influential component that shapes galaxies, yet remains a mystery and is entirely invisible to our most advanced telescopes. This dark matter is theorized to give galaxies extra mass, generating extra gravity that prevents them from tearing apart due to their rotational speeds\cite{cernDarkMatter}. Most dark matter searches usually operate underground, as seen in the SuperCDMS and LUX-ZEPLIN experiments, but the Earth's atmosphere and crust acts as a barrier that slows down or scatters X-ray signatures from dark matter decay.~\cite{CITE}

Charge Coupled Devices (CCDs) are silicon-based detectors which detect photons/ionizing radiation and convert it into a digital image or a quantitative dataset. These devices are primarily based on the photoelectric effect. The surface of a CCD is divided into squares called ``pixels'' and when a photon strikes a pixel, it knocks the electrons free, after which these charges are measured and converted into an image. Skipper CCDs are an advanced version of traditional CCDs and have significantly less readout noise compared to standard CCDs. In a standard CCD, the pixel charge is dumped onto the output node, measured once and wiped. However, a Skipper CCD has a modified output stage that allows this charge to be measured and ``skipped'' back and forth to be measured again, non-destructively.

The DarkNESS CubeSat mission uses Skipper Charge Coupled Devices (CCDs), which have demonstrated laboratory sensitivity to X-rays, to search for dark matter and demonstrate the detector technology in orbit. The 6U CubeSat operates in Low Earth Orbit (LEO). Its main scientific objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Search for strongly interacting sub-GeV dark matter.}
  For this search, DarkNESS targets Cygnus, near the direction of the solar system's motion through the Galactic dark matter halo. The resulting dark matter wind may produce a predictable daily modulation in the event rate as Earth shields the detector along its orbit.

  \item \textbf{Search for X-rays from dark matter decay.}
  For this search, DarkNESS points toward the Galactic Center, where the dark matter density is expected to be high, and looks for a monoenergetic X-ray from the decay of a keV-scale dark matter candidate, such as a sterile neutrino, into an active neutrino and a photon. Observations are scheduled during orbital umbra to reduce solar X-ray background contamination\cite{alpine2025darkness}.
\end{enumerate}

This paper separately considers two possible source channels for axion-like particles (ALPs), hypothetical pseudoscalar particles that may be connected to dark matter and early-universe physics. The cosmological channel is treated here as a diffuse flux of ALPs arriving from cosmological distances; the solar channel is a directional flux of ALPs produced in the Sun. In both channels, ALPs may convert into X-ray photons through the inverse Primakoff effect in Earth's magnetic field\cite{yamamoto2020alp,davoudiasl2008gecosax}. Thus, the channels differ in their origin and expected sky distribution but share the same conversion mechanism.

The purpose of this paper is to answer this research question:

\begin{quote}
``Which astrophysical ALP source channels can a LEO X-ray observatory identify through conversion in Earth’s magnetic field, and what instrument and observing strategy make those signals measurable against particle and diffuse X-ray backgrounds?''
\end{quote}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item How feasible is a cosmological ALP source channel for a LEO CubeSat using Skipper-CCDs, assuming conversion in Earth's magnetic field?
  \item How feasible is a solar ALP source channel for a LEO CubeSat using Skipper-CCDs, assuming conversion in Earth's magnetic field?
  \item How should the CubeSat's pointing strategy and observing constraints be set to observe whichever ALP channel is feasible?
  \item How should the CubeSat monitor backgrounds---including with proxies, control exposures, and a particle monitor---to distinguish a feasible channel's signal?
\end{enumerate}
